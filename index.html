Reanalysis of Paolini et al. (2024), Psychological Bulletin

How missing measures can look like a negativity bias

The published meta-analysis found that negative intergroup contact is more strongly linked to prejudice than positive contact. In its multilevel analysis of all effects, that difference depends on how the model links each sample's positive- and negative-contact effects. A thought experiment shows what that link does, and the data show the same pattern.

Part 1 · A thought experiment

The schools that skipped the math test

Every school in a district took the reading test; the weaker schools skipped the optional math test. Three models estimate the district's math average. They differ only in what they assume about how reading and math go together.

The link to contact

Why the published model implies a fixed total

The model gives each paper, study and sample a random intercept, which shifts all of that cluster's effects by the same amount. That is the standard way to handle several effects from one sample. But positive-contact effects are negative correlations (more positive contact, less prejudice), and negative-contact effects are positive. A shift that moves both the same way weakens one association and strengthens the other by the same amount, so in the model their combined strength is the same in every sample.

This holds for the model's true effects on the Fisher z scale; observed effects still vary through sampling error.

The model therefore treats every sample like a school with a fixed reading-plus-math total. Positive contact plays reading, negative contact plays math, and the 105 samples that measured only positive contact, which have weaker associations, are the schools that skipped the math test.

Part 2 · The meta-analytic data

The same pattern in the contact samples

Each point is one of the 191 samples in the revised pool, placed by the strength of its two associations with prejudice.

Positive-contact correlations are sign-reversed, so higher means stronger in the expected direction. Lines show the direction of each model's adjustment from the both-valence average.

How the estimates are computed
  • Schools. Bivariate random-effects meta-analysis with a between-school SD of 10 and a standard error of 5 for every score. The correlation between true reading and math scores is −1 (fixed total), 0 (no link) or estimated by maximum likelihood. Because of the standard errors, the fixed-total line is a little flatter than −1.
  • Contact. Three-level models (effects in samples in papers) fitted to Fisher z values of the 827 effects in the revised pool; the fixed total holds on that scale. The published fit to all 936 effects gives Δr = .080 (Supplement S3.1). Fitted means as in Supplement S3.3; Δr and 95% CIs (REML, CR2) as in Table S11. Each point is a sample's back-transformed mean z per valence; group averages weight samples equally.
  • No model imputes missing measurements. The assumed correlation decides how measurements of one kind inform the mean of the other.
Data: Paolini et al. (2024), revised pool as described in the Supplement.
